# PDPC Key Concepts in the PDPA - Curated Extract

Source: PDPC, *Advisory Guidelines on Key Concepts in the Personal Data Protection Act*, revised 29 April 2026. This is a curated extract prepared for a lightweight RAG assessment. It is not legal advice and does not replace the original guideline.

---

## 1. Purpose and overview of the PDPA
**Source pages: 7-9**

The Personal Data Protection Act 2012 (PDPA) governs the collection, use and disclosure of individuals' personal data by organisations in Singapore. The PDPA recognises both the right of individuals to protect their personal data and the need of organisations to collect, use and disclose personal data for purposes that a reasonable person would consider appropriate in the circumstances.

The PDPA contains two main sets of provisions:

1. **Data Protection Provisions** - these cover reasonable purposes, notification, consent, access and correction, accuracy, protection, retention, overseas transfers, data breach notification, and accountability.
2. **Do Not Call Provisions** - these cover certain marketing messages sent to Singapore telephone numbers.

The PDPA also includes offences for egregious mishandling of personal data, including knowing or reckless unauthorised disclosure, wrongful use, and re-identification of anonymised data.

---

## 2. What counts as personal data?
**Source pages: 11-16**

An individual under the PDPA means a natural person, whether living or deceased. The PDPA protects personal data of natural persons, not data relating only to companies or other legal entities.

Personal data is data, whether true or not, about an individual who can be identified:

1. from that data alone; or
2. from that data together with other information to which the organisation has, or is likely to have, access.

The definition is broad. Personal data can exist in electronic or non-electronic form. It does not matter whether the data is true or accurate; inaccurate data can still be personal data.

To decide whether data is personal data, consider:

- whether the information is about an individual;
- whether the individual is identifiable from the data alone or with other accessible information;
- the number and specificity of data elements;
- the nature of the data, such as whether it contains unique identifiers;
- the purpose of the dataset or document.

Some data points, such as NRIC or passport numbers, DNA, facial images, fingerprints, or iris prints, are more likely to identify an individual. Generic information such as age, gender, nationality or blood group may not identify a person on its own, but may become personal data when combined with other information.

A dataset may be personal data even when individual fields are not identifying by themselves. For example, age range, gender, occupation and place of work may identify a person if the combination is sufficiently unique.

## 3. Business contact information
**Source pages: 17-19**

The Data Protection Provisions do not apply to business contact information.

Business contact information includes an individual's name, position name or title, business telephone number, business address, business email address, business fax number, and similar information, provided that the information was not provided solely for personal purposes.

If someone provides a business name card at a corporate seminar to receive future seminar invitations, the information is likely to be business contact information. If the same person provides the business name card to sign up for a personal gym membership, the information is not business contact information because it was provided solely for personal purposes.

Contact information of sole proprietors and partners can also be business contact information if provided for business purposes.

---

## 4. Derived personal data
**Source page: 19**

Derived personal data refers to personal data about an individual that is derived by an organisation in the course of business from other personal data about the individual or another individual.

It generally refers to new data elements created through processing, such as mathematical, logical, statistical, computational, algorithmic or analytical methods based on business-specific rules.

Business insights or predictions about an identifiable individual may therefore be personal data.

---

## 5. Personal data of deceased individuals
**Source pages: 19-20**

The PDPA applies to personal data of deceased individuals to a limited extent.

For individuals who have been deceased for 10 years or less, the provisions relating to disclosure and protection of personal data continue to apply. This is intended to minimise adverse impact from unauthorised disclosure on family members of the deceased.

For individuals who have been deceased for more than 10 years, the PDPA does not apply to their personal data, although other legal or contractual requirements may still be relevant.

---

## 6. Control, not ownership
**Source pages: 20-21**

The PDPA does not confer ownership rights over personal data. Personal data refers to the information contained in the data, not necessarily the physical medium or document in which it is stored.

Individuals have some control rights under the PDPA, such as consent, withdrawal of consent, access and correction. However, ownership of a physical document, database, photograph or other medium is determined by other laws such as property law or copyright law.

Regardless of ownership, organisations must comply with the PDPA when collecting, using or disclosing personal data.

---

## 7. Organisations and excluded organisations
**Source pages: 22-24**

An organisation includes any individual, company, association or body of persons, corporate or unincorporated.

The Data Protection Provisions do not impose obligations on:

1. individuals acting in a personal or domestic capacity;
2. employees acting in the course of employment;
3. public agencies.

Although employees are excluded from direct obligations under the Data Protection Provisions when acting in the course of employment, organisations remain primarily responsible for their employees' actions.

---

## 8. Data intermediaries
**Source pages: 24-30**

A data intermediary is an organisation that processes personal data on behalf of another organisation, but does not include an employee of that organisation.

Where a data intermediary processes personal data on behalf of and for the purposes of another organisation under a written contract, the data intermediary is generally subject only to:

1. the Protection Obligation;
2. the Retention Limitation Obligation;
3. the Data Breach Notification Obligation, in relation to notifying the organisation of data breaches without undue delay.

The organisation using the data intermediary remains responsible for the personal data as if it had processed the data itself. Organisations should conduct appropriate due diligence and clearly define the data intermediary's scope of work, obligations and responsibilities in the written contract.

If a data intermediary uses or discloses personal data beyond the processing required by the contracting organisation, it may no longer be acting as a data intermediary for that activity and may have to comply with all Data Protection Provisions.

Organisations remain responsible for complying with the Transfer Limitation Obligation where a data intermediary transfers personal data overseas on their behalf.

## 9. Collection, use, disclosure, purpose and reasonableness
**Source pages: 31-33**

The PDPA does not define collection, use and disclosure exhaustively. In general:

- **Collection** means obtaining control over or possession of personal data.
- **Use** means employing personal data.
- **Disclosure** means making personal data available to another organisation.

Collection, use and disclosure can be active or passive.

A purpose refers to the organisation's objective or reason, not every activity that may be undertaken. For example, an organisation may state that personal data is collected for delivering purchased products, without listing every operational step such as entering data into a system or printing delivery labels.

Organisations must consider what a reasonable person would consider appropriate in the circumstances. Reasonableness is objective and context-specific.

## 10. The ten main Data Protection Obligations
**Source pages: 34-35**

The PDPA's Data Protection Provisions contain ten main obligations:

1. **Consent Obligation** - obtain consent before collecting, using or disclosing personal data, unless an exception or deemed consent applies.
2. **Purpose Limitation Obligation** - collect, use or disclose personal data only for purposes a reasonable person would consider appropriate and, where applicable, that have been notified.
3. **Notification Obligation** - notify individuals of the purposes for collection, use or disclosure.
4. **Access and Correction Obligations** - provide access to personal data and correct errors or omissions upon request, subject to exceptions.
5. **Accuracy Obligation** - make reasonable effort to ensure personal data is accurate and complete if used to make decisions affecting the individual or disclosed to another organisation.
6. **Protection Obligation** - make reasonable security arrangements to protect personal data.
7. **Retention Limitation Obligation** - cease retaining personal data, or remove the means of association with individuals, when the purpose is no longer served and retention is no longer necessary for legal or business purposes.
8. **Transfer Limitation Obligation** - transfer personal data overseas only in accordance with PDPA requirements.
9. **Data Breach Notification Obligation** - assess data breaches and notify affected individuals and/or the Commission where required.
10. **Accountability Obligation** - implement necessary policies and procedures and make information about them available.

## 11. Consent and valid consent
**Source pages: 38-42**

An individual has not given valid consent unless:

1. the individual has been notified of the purposes for which personal data will be collected, used or disclosed; and
2. the individual has provided consent for those purposes.

Consent may be express, verbal, or in some cases inferred from conduct. Written or accessible recorded consent provides the clearest indication.

Consent is not valid if the organisation requires consent, as a condition of providing a product or service, for collection, use or disclosure beyond what is reasonable to provide that product or service. Consent is also invalid if obtained through false or misleading information or deceptive practices.

Organisations may require consent where the personal data is reasonably required to provide the product or service.

---

## 12. Deemed consent
**Source pages: 42-51**

The PDPA recognises three forms of deemed consent:

1. **Deemed consent by conduct** - where an individual voluntarily provides personal data for purposes that are objectively obvious and reasonably appropriate in the circumstances.
2. **Deemed consent by contractual necessity** - where personal data is provided for a transaction and disclosure to downstream organisations is reasonably necessary to conclude or perform the transaction.
3. **Deemed consent by notification** - where an individual is notified of a new purpose, given a reasonable opt-out period and does not opt out, subject to required assessment and safeguards.

Deemed consent by conduct is limited to the purpose for which the individual voluntarily provided the data. For example, providing a credit card for payment does not mean consent for unrelated marketing.

Deemed consent by contractual necessity can extend through a transaction chain, such as payment processors, banks, delivery companies or logistics partners, where the sharing is reasonably necessary to perform the transaction.

Deemed consent by notification may be used for secondary purposes different from the original collection purpose, but the organisation must assess adverse effects, mitigate risks, provide adequate notification and provide a reasonable opt-out period. It cannot be used for sending direct marketing messages.

---

## 13. Withdrawal of consent
**Source pages: 54-60**

Individuals may withdraw consent at any time by giving reasonable notice.

Upon receiving a withdrawal notice, an organisation must:

1. inform the individual of the likely consequences of withdrawal;
2. not prohibit the individual from withdrawing consent;
3. cease collecting, using or disclosing the personal data for the withdrawn purpose unless collection, use or disclosure without consent is required or authorised under the PDPA or another written law;
4. ensure its data intermediaries and agents also cease the relevant collection, use or disclosure.

As a general rule of thumb, a withdrawal notice of at least 10 business days from receipt is considered reasonable notice.

Withdrawal of consent does not automatically require deletion or destruction of personal data. Organisations may retain personal data where permitted by the Retention Limitation Obligation, such as for legal or business purposes.

---

## 14. Exceptions to consent: legitimate interests
**Source pages: 60-68**

The legitimate interests exception allows collection, use or disclosure of personal data without consent where lawful interests of the organisation or another person outweigh any adverse effect on the individual.

To rely on the general legitimate interests exception, organisations must:

1. identify and articulate the legitimate interests;
2. conduct an assessment to identify adverse effects;
3. implement measures to eliminate, reduce or mitigate adverse effects;
4. conduct a balancing test where residual adverse effects remain;
5. disclose reliance on the legitimate interests exception, for example in a public data protection policy;
6. retain the assessment and provide it to the Commission upon request.

Examples of legitimate interests may include fraud detection, prevention of misuse of services, security, IT and network security, and necessary corporate due diligence.

Organisations cannot rely on the legitimate interests exception to send direct marketing messages.

---

## 15. Exceptions to consent: business improvement
**Source pages: 69-74**

The business improvement exception allows organisations to use personal data without consent for certain business improvement purposes, including:

1. improving, enhancing or developing new goods or services;
2. improving, enhancing or developing new methods or processes for business operations;
3. learning or understanding behaviour and preferences of individuals or groups;
4. identifying, personalising or customising goods or services for individuals.

To rely on this exception:

1. the purpose cannot reasonably be achieved without using personal data in individually identifiable form; and
2. the use must be one that a reasonable person would consider appropriate in the circumstances.

The exception may also apply to sharing between related corporations, subject to additional safeguards, contracts, agreements or binding corporate rules.

Organisations cannot rely on the business improvement exception to send direct marketing messages.

An example in the guideline states that a wearables company may use customer heart rate and step count data to train a machine learning model for a new health tracking feature where anonymised data is enough for model training, while historical personal data may be necessary for personalising the feature for each customer.

Another example states that a healthcare service provider and insurer in the same group may not rely on the business improvement exception to share identifiable medical records and payment information for automated claim assessment where identifiable data is not necessary and sharing medical information is unlikely to be considered appropriate to a reasonable person.

---

## 16. Exceptions to consent: research
**Source pages: 74-75**

The research exception allows use of personal data for a research purpose, including historical and statistical research, if:

1. the research cannot reasonably be accomplished unless the data is provided in individually identifiable form;
2. there is a clear public benefit;
3. the results will not be used to make decisions affecting individuals;
4. published results will not identify individuals.

Disclosure of personal data for research without consent has an additional condition: it must be impracticable to seek consent.

Mere inconvenience does not amount to impracticability. The organisation must be able to show that obtaining consent would be so onerous in cost, time or burden that the research is no longer viable, or that seeking consent would defeat or bias the research.

---

## 17. Purpose Limitation Obligation
**Source pages: 80-81**

An organisation may collect, use or disclose personal data only for purposes:

1. that a reasonable person would consider appropriate in the circumstances; and
2. where applicable, that have been notified to the individual.

The purpose must be relevant and reasonable. Broad or vague purposes such as "any other purpose that we deem fit" are unlikely to be considered reasonable or sufficiently specific.

---

## 18. Notification Obligation
**Source pages: 82-88**

Organisations must inform individuals of the purposes for collecting, using or disclosing personal data on or before collection, use or disclosure.

The PDPA does not prescribe a specific form of notification. Organisations should choose a method that gives the individual enough information to understand the purposes. Written or electronic notification is generally good practice.

Purposes should be stated at an appropriate level of detail. Organisations do not need to specify every operational activity, but they should avoid overly broad terms. For example, "valid business purposes" is not sufficiently specific.

Good practice includes:

- clear and accessible notices;
- headings or clear signposting;
- avoiding confusing legalistic language;
- layered notices where useful;
- highlighting unexpected or sensitive purposes;
- reviewing notification practices regularly.

If an organisation wants to use or disclose personal data for a new purpose, it should consider whether the new purpose is within the original notified scope, whether deemed consent applies, or whether an exception applies. If not, fresh consent is required.

---

## 19. Access and Correction Obligations
**Source pages: 90-109**

Upon request, an organisation must provide an individual with:

1. personal data about the individual that is in the possession or under the control of the organisation; and
2. information about how the personal data has been or may have been used or disclosed within one year before the request.

The organisation is not necessarily required to provide original documents or systems. It may provide the personal data in another appropriate form, and where feasible should provide only the relevant personal data rather than the whole document.

Organisations should verify the requester's identity before responding. If a third party makes the request on behalf of an individual, the organisation should ensure that the third party has legal authority to act for the individual.

If the organisation cannot respond within 30 days, it must inform the individual in writing within 30 days of when it will be able to respond.

Organisations may charge a reasonable fee based on incremental costs, but must provide a written estimate if they intend to charge a fee.

There are exceptions. For example, an organisation may not be required to provide access where the request is frivolous, vexatious, repetitious, disproportionately burdensome, or where information cannot be found. An organisation must not provide access where doing so could reveal another individual's personal data, threaten safety, cause immediate or grave harm, reveal a protected source's identity, or be contrary to national interest, subject to exceptions.

Where possible, organisations should provide access to the individual's personal data while redacting other individuals' personal data.

## 20. Accuracy Obligation
**Source pages: 110-113**

An organisation must make a reasonable effort to ensure personal data collected by or on behalf of the organisation is accurate and complete if the data is likely to be:

1. used by the organisation to make a decision affecting the individual; or
2. disclosed by the organisation to another organisation.

The degree of effort required depends on the circumstances, including the nature of the data, how it will be used and the possible impact on the individual.

---

## 21. Protection Obligation
**Source pages: 114-116**

An organisation must make reasonable security arrangements to protect personal data in its possession or under its control against unauthorised access, collection, use, disclosure, copying, modification, disposal or similar risks, and against loss of storage media or devices.

There is no one-size-fits-all approach. Reasonable security arrangements depend on factors such as:

- the nature of the personal data;
- the form of the data;
- the possible impact of unauthorised access or disclosure;
- the size and complexity of the organisation;
- operational needs.

Examples of security arrangements include administrative, physical and technical measures, such as policies, access controls, encryption, authentication, monitoring, training, and secure disposal.

---

## 22. Retention Limitation Obligation
**Source pages: 117-121**

An organisation must cease retaining documents containing personal data, or remove the means by which personal data can be associated with particular individuals, as soon as it is reasonable to assume that:

1. the purpose for which the data was collected is no longer being served by retention; and
2. retention is no longer necessary for legal or business purposes.

The PDPA does not prescribe a fixed retention period. Organisations should determine retention based on purpose, legal requirements and business needs.

An organisation may anonymise personal data as a way of ceasing to retain personal data, if the means of associating the data with particular individuals has been removed.

---

## 23. Transfer Limitation Obligation
**Source pages: 122-129**

An organisation must not transfer personal data outside Singapore except in accordance with PDPA requirements.

The organisation must ensure that transferred personal data receives a standard of protection comparable to the Data Protection Provisions under the PDPA, unless a prescribed exception applies.

Where a data intermediary transfers personal data overseas on behalf of an organisation, the organisation remains responsible for complying with the Transfer Limitation Obligation.

---

## 24. Data Breach Notification Obligation
**Source pages: 130-148**

An organisation must assess whether a data breach is notifiable and notify affected individuals and/or the Commission where required.

Once an organisation has credible grounds to believe a data breach has occurred, it must take reasonable and expeditious steps to assess whether the breach is notifiable.

A data breach is generally notifiable if it:

1. results in, or is likely to result in, significant harm to affected individuals; or
2. is of significant scale.

Data intermediaries that process personal data on behalf of another organisation must notify that organisation without undue delay when they have credible grounds to believe a data breach has occurred.

Where notification is required, organisations should provide information such as the circumstances of the breach, the personal data affected, the steps taken or planned, and steps affected individuals can take to protect themselves.

---

## 25. Accountability Obligation
**Source pages: 151-155**

An organisation must implement necessary policies and practices to meet its PDPA obligations and make information about those policies and practices publicly available.

Organisations must designate at least one individual as Data Protection Officer (DPO). The DPO is responsible for ensuring that the organisation complies with the PDPA. The business contact information of the DPO must be made available to the public.

Accountability measures may include:

- appointing a DPO;
- developing data protection policies and practices;
- making data protection policies publicly available;
- training employees;
- establishing complaint and enquiry processes;
- documenting decisions and assessments where required;
- reviewing policies and practices periodically.

## 26. Practical RAG-relevant distinctions in this extract

This document contains several distinctions that a RAG system should handle carefully:

1. **Personal data vs non-personal data** - identifiability depends on the dataset and other accessible information.
2. **Business contact information vs personal-purpose contact information** - same fields can be treated differently depending on purpose.
3. **Organisation vs data intermediary** - data intermediaries have limited obligations only when processing on behalf of another organisation under a written contract.
4. **Consent vs deemed consent vs exceptions** - these are different legal bases and should not be conflated.
5. **Deemed consent by conduct vs deemed consent by notification** - conduct applies to obvious purposes; notification requires assessment, notification and opt-out.
6. **Business improvement vs research** - business improvement relates to improving products, services or operations; research is broader and has public-benefit and publication conditions.
7. **Access to personal data vs access to original documents** - organisations may provide the data, not necessarily the original document.
8. **Withdrawal of consent vs deletion** - withdrawal does not automatically require deletion.
9. **Anonymisation vs retention** - anonymisation may remove association with individuals, but must be effective.
10. **Data breach by organisation vs data intermediary** - the data intermediary must notify the organisation without undue delay; the organisation assesses and handles notifiability.

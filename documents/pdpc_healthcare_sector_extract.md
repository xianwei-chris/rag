## Source page 3 - Introduction

These Guidelines should be read together with the PDPC's general guideline documents. Developed together with the Ministry of Health, they address circumstances faced by the healthcare sector in complying with the Personal Data Protection Act 2012 (PDPA).

---

## Source pages 4-5 - Consent, Purpose Limitation and Notification

The PDPA requires organisations to notify individuals of the purposes for collecting, using and disclosing personal data, and to obtain consent unless an exception applies. Organisations should only collect, use and disclose personal data that is relevant to the stated purposes, and for purposes that a reasonable person would consider appropriate in the circumstances.

### Deemed consent by conduct

Where an individual voluntarily provides personal data to an organisation for an appropriate purpose, and it is reasonable that the individual would voluntarily provide that data, consent may be deemed from the individual's conduct.

### Deemed consent by contractual necessity

If an individual gives or is deemed to have given consent to one organisation for a contractual transaction, that consent may cover sharing with other organisations where reasonably necessary to perform or conclude that contractual transaction.

### Deemed consent by notification

An individual may be deemed to have consented to use or disclosure for a notified secondary purpose if the individual does not opt out, provided the organisation meets the required conditions. The organisation must notify the individual of the purpose, assess adverse impact, and implement mitigating measures. Deemed consent by notification cannot be relied on if the individual opts out.

### Withdrawal of consent

Individuals may withdraw consent for collection, use or disclosure of their personal data. However, an organisation may continue to use or disclose personal data in its possession if allowed under other legal bases.

### Exceptions to consent

The PDPA permits collection, use or disclosure of personal data without consent for specific purposes set out in the PDPA. These exceptions do not override obligations under other laws, confidentiality duties, or contractual obligations.

## Source pages 6-8 - Key Exceptions to Consent

### Legitimate interests exception

Organisations may collect, use or disclose personal data without consent where the identified legitimate interests outweigh adverse effects on the individual. Organisations relying on this exception must assess and address adverse effects, including eliminating, reducing, or mitigating them. The legitimate interests exception cannot be used to send direct marketing messages.

### Business improvement exception

Organisations may use personal data without consent for business improvement purposes, such as improving goods or services, improving business processes, understanding behaviour and preferences, or identifying suitable goods and services. To rely on this exception, the purpose cannot reasonably be achieved without using personal data in individually identifiable form, and the use must be one a reasonable person would consider appropriate. The business improvement exception cannot be used to send direct marketing messages.

### Research exception

Organisations may use personal data for research without consent where the research purpose cannot reasonably be accomplished unless the personal data is provided in individually identifiable form, there is a clear public benefit, the results will not be used to make decisions affecting the individual, and any published results will not identify the individual. Disclosure for research also requires that it is impracticable to seek consent.

## Source pages 9-11 - Collecting Patient Data for Medical Care

John visits Hospital ABC for the first time for a medical examination. He fills in a registration form with his full name, address, NRIC number and mobile number.

By voluntarily providing his personal data, including by presenting himself for medical examination, John may be deemed to have consented to the collection, use and disclosure of his personal data for the purpose of his visit, including medical care related to the visit.

Depending on the circumstances, this may include associated examinations or tests, follow-up consultations related to the purpose of the visit, and a case conference with other doctors within Hospital ABC solely to discuss treatment options for John.

Deemed consent by conduct does not cover purposes outside those for which the personal data was provided. If Hospital ABC wants to use John's personal data for purposes unrelated to medical care, it is less likely to be covered by deemed consent by conduct. Hospital ABC should notify John and obtain consent unless another basis applies.

For example, using John's personal data to market health products unrelated to his condition is unlikely to be covered by deemed consent from his visit. Hospital ABC should actively obtain express consent for this purpose.

Hospital ABC should not require consent to collect, use or disclose personal data beyond what is reasonable to provide the medical service. As good practice, it should not collect more personal data than required for business or legal purposes and should mark which form fields are compulsory or optional.

Hospital ABC may retain John's personal data after his visit if there is a legal or business purpose, including healthcare-sector rules on medical record retention.

Where doctors, medical students, employees or volunteers are involved in providing medical care as part of the purpose of the visit, separate consent is not needed for those activities if they fall within the medical care purpose.

## Source pages 11-12 - Referrals

Doctor Lee recommends that Patient A consult a specialist, Patient B visit a hospital for further tests, and Patient C consider long-term care services. Each patient verbally agrees and Doctor Lee makes the necessary arrangements, such as contacting another doctor directly.

Since each patient agreed to the recommendation, the patient would have consented to the doctor disclosing personal data as required for the referral. If Doctor Lee gives the patient a referral letter and the patient takes it to the receiving organisation, the patient is considered to have disclosed the personal data to that organisation.

As good practice, Doctor Lee could document verbal consent in the patient file. Before disclosing the patients' personal data, Doctor Lee should take reasonable steps to ensure the personal data is accurate and complete, and comply with applicable healthcare requirements and licensing conditions.

Doctor Lee may disclose the personal data pursuant to such consent regardless of whether or when the patients arrive at the facility to which they were referred.

## Source pages 12-13 - Family Medical History and Other Individuals' Data

During John's consultation, the doctor asks if John has a family history of cancer. John says his Aunt Kim had stomach cancer. This may or may not be personal data of Aunt Kim, depending on whether Aunt Kim can be identified from the data itself or when combined with other data the organisation has or is likely to access.

If the doctor asks for Aunt Kim's full name and treatment institution because it is relevant to John's care, the organisation is likely collecting personal data about Aunt Kim. The organisation may collect Aunt Kim's personal data without her consent where the data was provided by John to enable the organisation to provide a service for John's personal or domestic purpose, namely medical care for John. The organisation remains obliged to comply with other PDPA obligations, such as protecting Aunt Kim's personal data.

If Aunt Kim cannot be identified from the information or from likely accessible information, the data is not personal data and the PDPA does not apply. The organisation must make that assessment.

## Source pages 14-15 - Uses Beyond the Patient Visit

Health Organisation ABC wants to use John's personal data for internal quality assurance and for teaching material.

Generally, Health Organisation ABC should notify John and obtain consent for additional purposes unless a relevant exception applies.

For internal quality assurance and activities integral to the proper functioning of business operations that support the delivery of medical care, Health Organisation ABC is unlikely to need to specifically notify John or obtain consent.

For teaching material, Health Organisation ABC should typically notify John and obtain consent if the data cannot be anonymised. Consent is not required where training or professional registration activities do not involve collection, use or disclosure of personal data, such as where a trainee doctor records only the number of hours spent performing a procedure and the information does not identify any patient.

Health Organisation ABC cannot require John to consent to additional purposes as a condition of medical care if those purposes are not reasonably required to provide medical care. A contractual obligation to teach students or formulate teaching materials cannot be made a requirement for providing medical care to John.

As good practice, Health Organisation ABC should consider whether the purpose can be achieved without using personal data, such as by using anonymised datasets. Consent is not required if personal data is anonymised before use and the anonymised data does not relate to any identifiable individual.

## Source pages 15-16 - Emergency Use

John takes his father to Clinic ABC because his father has a very high fever. During examination, John's father collapses. Clinic ABC calls an ambulance and discloses his father's personal data to the hospital and ambulance services.

Clinic ABC and the hospital may collect, use and disclose John's father's personal data without consent to respond to an emergency that threatens his life or health. The hospital should notify John's father as soon as practicable of the collection, use or disclosure and the purpose.

## Source page 16 - Consent Covers Activities for the Purpose

Organisations must notify individuals of purposes and obtain consent unless an exception applies. However, when specifying purposes, an organisation does not need to specify every activity it may undertake. It should specify its objectives or reasons for collecting, using or disclosing personal data.

If Healthcare Institution XYZ has obtained John's consent to collect, use and disclose personal data to other healthcare institutions for providing medical treatment, it need not separately obtain consent to maintain medical records in a database or disclose relevant records through the database if those activities are undertaken for the consented purpose.

## Source pages 16-18 - Employees, Locums and Practice Acquisition

The PDPA Data Protection Provisions do not impose obligations on employees acting in the course of employment with an organisation. Acts by an employee in the course of employment are treated as acts of the employer for PDPA purposes. The organisation must ensure compliance, though individuals may be accountable for egregious mishandling of personal data or for conduct outside the course of employment.

For a locum doctor, the relevant obligations depend on the arrangement, including whether the locum processes personal data for the clinic under a written contract or is engaged as an employee.

If a medical practice is acquired by another organisation, the business asset transaction exception may apply. A buyer may collect, use or disclose patient personal data without consent to the extent the personal data relates directly to the relevant business asset transaction, subject to the conditions of the exception.

## Source pages 18-19 - Research Without Consent

Health Organisation ABC wants to conduct retrospective research using old medical records from research and administrative databases to understand disease epidemiology and socio-demographic characteristics that could influence public health strategies. The purpose cannot reasonably be accomplished unless personal data is provided in individually identifiable form. The organisation no longer has contact details and does not know whether the patients have passed away or relocated, so it would be impracticable to seek consent. The results will not be used to make decisions affecting individuals, and the benefits are clearly in the public interest.

In this case, Health Organisation ABC may use personal data without consent under the research exception. If it publishes the results, the results must be represented in a form that does not identify the past patients. Health Organisation ABC may also take into account the opinion of an Institutional Review Board or equivalent body.

## Source pages 20-22 - Access and Correction Requests

The Access and Correction Obligations require an organisation, upon request, to provide an individual with personal data in its possession or under its control, and information about how the personal data has been or may have been used or disclosed during the past year. The organisation must also correct an error or omission in the individual's personal data in its possession or under its control.

Before responding to an access request, the clinic should verify the individual's identity. The clinic may ask the individual to be more specific to facilitate processing or determine whether an exception applies.

The clinic does not necessarily have to provide copies of original documents such as registration forms or doctor's notes. It should provide the personal data in an appropriate form. For example, if John requests a diagnosis recorded in handwritten notes, the clinic is not obliged to provide a photocopy of the handwritten notes, though it should provide the requested information, such as through a medical report, unless an exception applies.

For use and disclosure information, the clinic must provide information about the ways personal data has been or may have been used or disclosed within one year before the request date, unless an exception applies. The clinic may provide a standard updated list of routine parties to whom personal data is used or disclosed.

The clinic may charge a reasonable fee and must respond as soon as reasonably possible. If it cannot respond within 30 days, it must inform the individual in writing within that 30-day period when it will be able to respond.

If the clinic rejects an access request based on an exception, it must reply and state the relevant reasons. It must preserve a complete and accurate copy of the personal data for at least 30 calendar days after rejection because the individual may seek a review.

For correction requests, it would be reasonable to correct contact details to keep them accurate and current. The clinic may decide not to correct smoking habit records if satisfied on reasonable grounds that correction need not be made. Where a diagnosis is a professional or expert opinion, the clinic is not required to correct or alter it. If the clinic does not make a requested correction, it should annotate the personal data with the correction requested but not made.

## Source pages 23-24 - Accuracy, Protection, Retention, Transfer, Breach and Accountability

### Accuracy

An organisation must make a reasonable effort to ensure personal data collected by or on its behalf is accurate and complete if the personal data is likely to be used to make a decision affecting the individual, or disclosed to another organisation.

### Protection

An organisation must protect personal data in its possession or under its control by making reasonable security arrangements to prevent unauthorised access, collection, use, disclosure, copying, modification, disposal or similar risks, and to prevent loss of storage media or devices containing personal data.

### Retention

An organisation must cease to retain documents containing personal data, or remove the means by which the data can be associated with individuals, once it is reasonable to assume the original purpose is no longer served and retention is no longer necessary for legal or business purposes. The PDPA does not prescribe a specific retention period. Healthcare institutions should regularly review whether personal data is still needed and should not keep personal data "just in case".

### Transfer

An organisation must not transfer personal data outside Singapore except in accordance with PDPA requirements.

### Data breach notification

An organisation must assess whether a data breach is notifiable and notify affected individuals and/or the Commission where it is assessed to be notifiable.

### Accountability

An organisation must implement necessary policies and procedures to meet its PDPA obligations and make information about its policies and procedures publicly available.

## Source pages 25-26 - Other Laws and Pre-PDPA Data

The PDPA does not affect authority, rights, privileges, immunities, obligations or limitations under other laws unless otherwise provided. Where another written law is inconsistent with the PDPA Data Protection Provisions, the other written law prevails to the extent of the inconsistency. Healthcare organisations should continue to comply with laws such as the PHMC Act, HCSA, National Registry of Diseases Act, Infectious Diseases Act and Advance Medical Directive Act.

For example, if a medical practitioner is required by the Infectious Diseases Act to notify the Director of Medical Services of an infectious disease, the practitioner does not need consent under the PDPA to make that notification.

An organisation may use personal data collected before 2 July 2014 for the purposes for which it was collected, unless consent is withdrawn or the individual has indicated they do not consent. The Do Not Call Provisions still apply to specified messages sent to Singapore telephone numbers even if the numbers were collected before that date.

## Source pages 27-30 - Do Not Call Provisions

Messages whose purpose is to offer, advertise or promote goods, services, land, business or investment opportunities are specified messages and the Do Not Call (DNC) Provisions apply. Messages without such purposes are not specified messages. Some excluded messages include transaction-related messages, messages sent in an ongoing relationship where the sole purpose relates to that relationship, and messages solely for market research or surveys.

Clinic ABC calls John solely to confirm he completed medication, check that his fever has subsided, or make an appointment to review test results. These are unlikely to be specified messages. A dental appointment reminder is also unlikely to be a specified message where its sole purpose is to remind the patient of an appointment.

An organisation sending a specified message must check the DNC Registry unless the user or subscriber has given clear and unambiguous consent in written or other accessible form.

Failure to opt out is unlikely, by itself, to constitute clear and unambiguous consent for marketing calls. For example, if Clinic ABC writes to former patients saying they will be deemed to consent to supplement marketing calls unless they opt out, Peter's failure to reply is unlikely to be clear and unambiguous consent. Clinic ABC must check the DNC Register before calling him to market the supplement.

If Jason checks a box in a patient registration form consenting to reminder text messages for future dental visits, this is clear and unambiguous consent. In addition, a reminder message whose sole purpose is to facilitate a transaction Jason previously agreed to may be sent without checking the DNC Registry.

For patients with chronic conditions, whether a message is excluded depends on whether the new drug or procedure relates to a condition for which the clinic is providing ongoing treatment. A message to John about a new asthma drug while John is receiving ongoing asthma treatment may be excluded from the definition of specified message. A message to Sarah about asthma, where Sarah has never sought asthma-related treatment from the clinic, would require DNC compliance unless clear and unambiguous consent was obtained. Even if Sarah is being treated for another condition such as migraines, the clinic must comply with DNC rules when sending a marketing message about an unrelated condition such as asthma.

Organisations must not send or authorise messages to telephone numbers obtained by dictionary attack or address-harvesting software.
